Field firmware update for IP intercom nodes

Made remote firmware updates possible for VoIP intercom nodes in secure facilities, where devices cannot be reached by hand and site staff update them over the live network. I extended the Windows maintenance tool with network firmware update, per-input event reporting and codec selection, designing each change against the node firmware and hardware on two processor families.

  • One tool now updates four device variants on two processor families, node by node, and reports exactly which nodes failed, so site staff know what still runs old firmware.
  • Proved the update cannot brick a node: cut power and pulled the Ethernet link mid-transfer on both hardware families, and every node fell back to its working image through the dual-bank flash fail-safe.
  • Studied the node hardware from its schematics before changing any software: a Cortex-M7 controller behind an on-board Ethernet switch, audio codec ICs on I2S and I2C, bridge-tied-load speaker amplifiers and six field inputs with configurable trigger level and debounce time.
  • Mapped the node firmware's command path end to end: LwIP UDP callbacks feeding a receive ring buffer, a command dispatcher, a retrying transmit queue and settings held as C structures in internal flash and read back after every write.
  • Traced both update paths through the node firmware, an in-application bank write with XOR checksum validation on the dsPIC33E and a boot-loader handover on the STM32F7 and used that analysis to replace the tool's built-in download with the proven production loader.
  • Added features to a fixed length UDP protocol without a single new command: the codec as an RTP payload type in a spare data word (G.711 at 8 kHz or G.722 at 16 kHz) and six input reporting flags in an existing byte, kept backward compatible with nodes already installed.
  • Root-caused three long-standing configuration bugs from packet captures and firmware source, including a MAC address command that used the read sub-command instead of the write sub-command, so nodes acknowledged and changed nothing. Verified every fix by power-cycling the node and reading the setting back from flash.
  • Diagnosed poor field audio as a codec and sample-rate mismatch and proposed and built the codec selection that fixed it.
  • Wrote the illustrated update manual for site staff and added per-variant file checks in front of the firmware's own hardware-version interlock.

Hardware · Fault analysis · STM32F4 / STM32F7 · Ethernet PHY · Production test

Ethernet failure investigation and production quality

Traced a network failure across multiple access controllers to counterfeit Ethernet switch ICs, specified a released design change to the lock-drive outputs and took over fault-finding, test and release of two embedded products, an access controller and a VoIP intercom node, both built for secure facilities where a faulty unit is a safety risk.

  • Isolated the fault from multiple units that had dropped off the network, some only after weeks in service: they still answered over Bluetooth, which runs on an RS-232 serial path independent of Ethernet, proving the controller, firmware and supply were healthy and confining the search to the Ethernet path.
  • Compared failing boards circuit by circuit against a known-good earlier build, eliminating every cause that mimics a dead PHY: 3.3 V and 1.8 V rails after the ferrite-bead filters, the 25 MHz crystal and MII clocks, reset release and strap-pin levels, the ISET bias resistor, the MDC/MDIO management bus, and the magnetics, terminations and isolation capacitors.
  • With everything around it correct, the three-port switch IC itself ran hot on the network, pointing to excess current inside the device. The parts carried different markings and a lower temperature grade than the specified industrial part: counterfeit, confirmed by the supplier. Escalated to procurement and management; after a further supplier change the failures stopped.
  • Reworked boards myself, replacing the 48-pin LQFP device by hot-air rework with pin-by-pin inspection and a full rail, clock and dual-port link re-test, then trained the workshop on the method and inspected their rework of the rest.
  • Specified a released design change when a client's higher-current electromagnetic lock would not hold reliably, with the gate drive clamped at 3.6 V, a 4.7 kΩ gate resistor left the low-side MOSFET only partly enhanced. Changed it to 0 Ω on all six lock-drive channels, kept the gate clamp and current limit in place, and had the BOM and schematic revised for production.
  • Ended a two-week production stoppage on the test jig, where two in five units failed at random and the team was working overtime retesting until units passed: found an intermittent partial short, loose connectors and wiring that no longer matched the drawing. Replaced the faulty board, rewired and labelled the jig to its diagram, and swapped fatigue-prone pogo pins for plug-in connectors. False failures dropped to zero, exposing the real soldering defects behind the noise.
  • Repaired returned units at component level from the schematics, tracing surge damage through PTC fuses, varistors, TVS diodes and buck regulators, opto-isolated MOSFET lock drivers and Class-AB audio stages, and confirming a dead microcontroller by commanding its GPIO and probing the pins.
  • Held sign-out authority for both products as QA: every unit leaving the workshop needed my release and I rejected units that would have been unreliable or unsafe on site, regardless of delivery pressure.
